Why Does m_b Saturate Near 6.5?

m_b samples vertical P waves around T = 1 s (f = 1 Hz) in the first few wave cycles. Once M_W exceeds ~4.5–5.5, corner frequency f_c drops below 1 Hz, placing 1 Hz on the ω⁻² rolloff where A(1 Hz) ∝ M₀^(1/3) instead of M₀¹. Furthermore, great earthquakes rupture for tens to hundreds of seconds, so a few-second window misses most of the seismic moment.

Why Does M_S Saturate Near 8.0?

M_S measures 20 s Rayleigh waves (f = 0.05 Hz). Because 0.05 Hz is 20× lower than 1 Hz, M_S remains accurate longer—matching M_W closely between M 6.5 and 7.8. However, for M_W ≥ 8.0, rupture duration exceeds 20 s (f_c < 0.05 Hz), and 20 s wavelengths (~70 km) interfere destructively across 200–1300 km fault ruptures, capping M_S around 8.3–8.5.

Why Does M_W Never Saturate?

M_W is defined directly from the static scalar seismic moment M₀ = μ D̄ A (Kanamori 1977; Hanks & Kanamori 1979), inferred from ultra-long-period waves (T ≫ τ_d, f ≪ f_c) on the flat low-frequency plateau Ω₀ ∝ M₀, or from static GPS/InSAR fault slip models. Every unit increase in M_W faithfully represents 10^1.5 ≈ 31.6× more seismic moment.

Why the High-Frequency Slope Is ω⁻² (Eqs. 9.33 & 9.36)

In the Haskell (1964) rectangular fault model, the far-field displacement pulse is a trapezoid produced by convolving two boxcars: rise time τ_r and rupture duration τ_d. Each boxcar contributes a sinc(ωτ/2) factor whose envelope decays as ω⁻¹ above its corner frequency. Convolving both yields ω⁰ (f < f_c1), ω⁻¹ (f_c1 < f < f_c2), and ω⁻² at high frequencies. Brune (1970) smoothed this into the canonical single-corner ω⁻² spectrum:

A(f) = Ω₀ / [1 + (f / fc)2]   (Brune, Eq. 9.36)

Algebraic Proof of Spectral Saturation Exponent 1/3

Take a fixed measurement frequency f₀ (1 Hz for m_b or 0.05 Hz for M_S). When an earthquake becomes huge so that f_c ≪ f₀, the denominator is dominated by (f₀/f_c)². Substituting Ω₀ ∝ M₀ and self-similar f_c ∝ M₀^(-1/3):

A(f₀) ≈ Ω₀ · (fc / f₀)² ∝ M₀ · (M₀−1/3)² = M₀1/3
Therefore log₁₀ A(f₀) grows at only 0.33 log₁₀ M₀ = 0.5 M_W — half the rate of M_W, causing severe underestimation!
